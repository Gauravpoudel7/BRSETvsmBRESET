# Research proposal introduction

Diabetic retinopathy is eye damage caused by diabetes. If it is caught late, people can go blind. Screening means taking a photo of the back of the eye and checking it for signs of disease. In rich countries this often happens with a big, expensive camera in a clinic. In poorer places, and in community screening events, doctors increasingly use a small handheld camera that works like a phone attachment. That is good for access, but the pictures look different: different lighting, blur, colour, and framing. AI models are now used to read these photos automatically. Many of the best models, called foundation models, were trained mostly on clinic-grade photos from hospitals in Europe and North America. A real product question follows: if we plug one of these off-the-shelf models into a portable camera in Brazil, can we trust it? And if it makes mistakes, do those mistakes hit everyone equally, or do they fall harder on older people, women, or patients who are poorer or have less education? That second question matters for fairness, because screening is meant to help the people who need it most, not skip over them.

A structured review of 39 recent papers shows the problem is real, and partly already measured. When AI trained on clinic photos is tested on handheld photos, performance usually drops, sometimes by a lot. A 2026 study already did this exact test on two public Brazilian datasets: BRSET (clinic cameras in Sao Paulo) and mBRSET (handheld Phelcom Eyer camera in Bahia). Scores fell clearly when models moved from clinic to handheld images. So the basic “does it still work?” question is largely answered: often no, unless you adapt the model with data from the new device. What is not answered is whether the drop is the same for every kind of patient. A separate study looked at fairness only on handheld photos and found gaps by age and other traits. Nobody has yet asked the joined question: when accuracy falls because the camera changed, does that fall land unevenly across patient groups? The datasets also differ in more than the camera (different cities, different rates of diabetes and eye disease, different graders), so a careful study must be honest about what it can prove.

This project will train one or more retinal foundation models (such as RETFound) on clinic-grade photos, then evaluate them on handheld photos without retraining, matching the real-world “borrowed model” scenario. The main contribution will be breaking results down by patient subgroups where the data allow it: age and sex on both datasets, and education and insurance on the handheld side. We will report not only overall accuracy but also fairness-style comparisons, with confidence intervals, and we will flag subgroups that are too small to draw firm conclusions from. As a control, we will also compare the two clinic cameras inside BRSET (Canon vs Nikon) to estimate how much of any drop is “camera only” versus “different patients and disease rates.” The outcome will inform both scholarship and a practical decision: whether a healthcare device company can rely on an off-the-shelf model on its own camera, or must collect device-specific data and fine-tune. The literature suggests fine-tuning on the target device is often necessary; this study will show who pays the price if that step is skipped.

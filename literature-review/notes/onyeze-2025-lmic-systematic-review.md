# Effectiveness of AI-Based Tools in Detecting Diabetic Retinopathy in Low- and Middle-Income Countries: A Systematic Review of Diagnostic Performance and Implementation Feasibility

**Citation:** Onyeze N, Sartawi S, Nayyer Z. Effectiveness of AI-Based Tools in Detecting Diabetic Retinopathy in Low- and Middle-Income Countries: A Systematic Review of Diagnostic Performance and Implementation Feasibility. Cureus. 2025. doi: 10.7759/cureus.96554. Venue: Cureus. **Peer reviewed.**
**Year:** 2025
**Dataset(s) used:** Studies published between January 2015 and June 2025, found across six databases. Systems included AIDRSS (India), a bespoke algorithm (Zambia), the Google and Thai Ministry of Public Health system (Thailand), an AI triage workflow (Rwanda), embedded AI on the Phelcom Eyer handheld camera (Brazil), and offline Medios AI with a Remidio camera. Sample sizes ranged from 275 in a Rwandan trial to 7,651 in a Thai national programme.
**Model(s)/method(s):** Systematic review of diagnostic and implementation outcomes. No new model built.

## Summary (plain English)
This review asks whether AI screening for diabetic retinopathy actually works in poorer countries, not just in labs. Low- and middle-income countries, often shortened to LMICs, are where most of the disease burden is and where eye doctors are scarcest. The authors searched six databases over a ten-year period and kept only studies reporting real numbers on either accuracy or how deployment went. They deliberately excluded studies limited to rich countries.

## Key finding (plain English)
Only a small number of studies qualified. Across those, accuracy was generally high and deployment was feasible, with signs of lower costs, less clinician workload and better patient follow-up. Reporting on infrastructure needs, regulation and long-term sustainability was thin.

## Relevance to my research (plain English)
**Supports my gap and frames why the question matters.** The headline point for me is scarcity: after ten years and six databases, only a handful of qualifying studies exist for the settings where this technology is needed most. That is a strong argument that work on mBRSET-style data is filling a real hole. It also explicitly lists the Phelcom Eyer with embedded AI in Brazil as one of the few deployment examples, which places my target device in the global evidence base. As a Cureus paper it carries less weight than the npj Digital Medicine meta-analysis, so I should use it for context and the LMIC framing rather than as my main statistical evidence.

# Real-World Evaluation of AI-Driven Diabetic Retinopathy Screening in Public Health Settings: Validation and Implementation Study

**Citation:** Duggal M, Chauhan A, Gupta V, Kankaria A, Budhija D, Verma P, Miglani V, Syal P, Kaur G, Kumar L, Mutyala N, Bezbaruah R, Sood N, Kernohan A, Menon G, Vale L. Real-World Evaluation of AI-Driven Diabetic Retinopathy Screening in Public Health Settings: Validation and Implementation Study. JMIR Medical Informatics. 2025;13:e67529. doi: 10.2196/67529. Venue: JMIR Medical Informatics. **Peer reviewed.**
**Year:** 2025
**Dataset(s) used:** Fundus images from 250 people with diabetes for validation in Chandigarh Tricity, north India, then 343 patients at a community health centre in Moga district, Punjab. Low-cost non-mydriatic cameras, meaning no dilating eye drops.
**Model(s)/method(s):** Three commercial AI screening algorithms prospectively validated against human graders, then the best one integrated into a real screening workflow.

## Summary (plain English)
This study did something unusually honest. The team invited five AI companies to have their products tested on images from low-cost cameras in ordinary public clinics. One declined and one withdrew partway through after interim results showed poor specificity. The three that stayed were then tested prospectively, meaning on new patients as they arrived rather than on a stored dataset. The best performer was then installed at a real community health centre and used on hundreds more patients.

## Key finding (plain English)
The three algorithms varied hugely, with sensitivity between 60% and 80% and specificity between 14% and 96%. The best one, once deployed, reached 68% sensitivity, 96% specificity and 88.43% accuracy, and at the health centre detected referable disease with 79% sensitivity.

## Relevance to my research (plain English)
**Strongly supports my gap and is the most sobering paper here for the product decision.** Commercial AI products that presumably reported strong numbers in their own validations produced specificity as low as 14% on real low-cost camera images in a public clinic. That is the practical consequence of the domain shift my study measures, seen in a deployment rather than a benchmark. It also shows that a vendor's published accuracy tells you almost nothing about performance on your own device in your own setting, which is precisely the argument for my company running its own validation. The fact that a company withdrew after seeing interim results is itself worth noting.
